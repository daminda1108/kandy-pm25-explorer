How it works

← Back to explorer

The Kandy PM2.5 Explorer reconstructs a 1 km, hourly fine-particulate field over the Kandy valley for 2019–2026. Kandy's monitoring record is sparse, so the model combines satellite products, reanalysis meteorology, terrain physics and ground measurements from comparable cities, a transfer-validation approach explained below. The satellite-anchored years (2019–2023) carry the headline; 2024–2026 are a labelled extension. This page describes what each part of the map is based on and how much weight it can carry.

The model in one equation

PM(x, y, t) = B(t) + [ T(t) − B(t) ] · Plocal(x, y, t)
  • T(t) is the basin-mean level for each hour. It comes from a gradient-boosted model driven by ERA5 meteorology, GEOS-CF and CAMS chemistry, satellite AOD and NO₂, and solar radiation. Each year it is re-anchored to the satellite-derived annual mean, and its diurnal and seasonal swing is adjusted to match ground observations.
  • B(t) is the regional background that arrives over the area regardless of local activity. It is estimated from a rural satellite floor, shaped by the seasonal cycle and conditioned on air-mass origin.
  • Plocal(x, y, t) is the within-valley spatial pattern (mean 1): a traffic-emission surface modulated by boundary-layer confinement and mass-consistent WindNinja terrain winds (channelling, night-time drainage, day-time mixing).

The basin mean of the field equals T(t) at every hour, so the whole animation can be rebuilt in the browser from a few small arrays. The physics was computed once, offline; nothing is fetched from a server while you scrub.

Local sources emit continuously, so the regional background can never exceed the total; capping it there gives a local fraction of 0.48, and that value barely moves (0.477–0.502) across a fourfold sweep of the one free parameter. It replaces an earlier literature-based estimate of about 25%, which this constraint refutes. Kandy is relatively clean in itself and episodically loaded by long-range transport.

How the method is validated: proving-ground cities

Kandy has no dense monitoring network, so the fine-scale field shown here cannot be checked against local stations directly. Instead we run the same method, blind, on cities that do have dense networks: we build the map as if the city had almost no ground data, then score it against the monitors that were deliberately withheld from the model. Reconstructing a monitored city the model has never seen is evidence that it is doing something real when applied to Kandy, this is the transfer-validation logic that underpins the whole approach.

The flagship proving ground is Medellín, Colombia, a tropical Andean valley with a complete PM2.5 record and a Kandy-analogue regime. Run blind against its withheld network, the identical engine reproduces the seasonal cycle (r ≈ 0.99), the daily cycle (r ≈ 0.89) and the station-by-station spatial ranking (ρ ≈ 0.78, the best in the panel), with the level landing within about 6%. The same machinery has been tested across a nine-city panel spanning five countries and three continents. Improvements are proven on these monitored cities first, and only the ones that hold up across several of them are ported to Kandy, the wind recalibration and the post-2023 level extension used in this explorer were both validated on Medellín before being brought here.

Extending past the satellite anchor (2024–2026)

The annual level for 2019–2023 is anchored to a satellite product whose current release ends in 2023. A simple chemistry-model scaling proved unreliable for carrying it forward, it misses the emission anomalies of 2021 (pandemic) and 2022 (fuel crisis). So for 2024 onward we replaced the anchor with a model trained on meteorology and a global chemistry forecast: the same driver-anchored approach we validated on Medellín, where (trained only on data through 2023) it reproduced the 2024 annual level to within a few percent of monitors it had never seen. The 2024–2026 years are shown as a separate, clearly labelled extension tier: the level and timing are modelled, the street-scale pattern is the 2019–2023 climatology, and the level carries a wider uncertainty. The locked 2019–2023 headline is untouched by the extension.

One defect in this tier is worth stating plainly, because we found it late. The statistical model behind it systematically under-produced high-pollution hours: before August 2026 it essentially could not generate an episode, reporting under one hour a year above 55 µg/m³ where the anchored years contain roughly eighty. A correction fitted on the anchored years (and checked by holding each of them out in turn) restores the frequency and size of episodes to within a few percent of the truth. It fixes how often episodes of a given size occur. It does not establish that they fall on the right hours: the model's ranking of which hours are dirty is unchanged by the correction. Read a specific hour in 2024–2026 as indicative of conditions, not as a reading.

A second caveat applies to every year, not just the extension. The hour-to-hour sharpness of the map (how much dirtier the traffic core looks than the ridges at a given moment) is set partly by a bounded constant that has never been calibrated against measurements. On calm nights and at rush hour, when that bound is reached, the map can show a core several times the basin average. The direction is right and the annual average contrast (about 1.2×) is the figure we would defend; a single dramatic hour is not.

The five-day forecast, a demonstration, not a checked product

Dates beyond the reconstructed record are selectable in the date picker and marked with a ▸. They come from the same frozen Kandy model, driven by NASA's GEOS-CF chemistry forecast instead of reanalysis. Two things about them matter more than the numbers themselves.

Nothing in the Kandy forecast is verified locally. Kandy has no public in-basin monitoring station, so there is no observation to score a forecast against and none of the usual claims of forecast accuracy can be made here. What can be shown is the method's performance where a network does exist: run at Medellín with the anchor trained only on 2019–2022 and scored on 2023 against 15 withheld stations, it reaches an RMSE of 5.71 µg/m³ against 6.49 for 24-hour persistence (a skill of +0.120) and retains the seasonal (r 0.974) and daily (r 0.965) shape. That is a Medellín number in a locally-dominated pollution regime; Kandy's is regional and transboundary, so it bounds the method, not this city's forecast. An earlier version of that backtest reported roughly double the skill; it had trained on hours inside its own evaluation year, and the corrected figure is the one quoted here.

The interval is the claim, not the middle value. Forecast hours show a 90% range first and the most-likely value second, and the range is widened by a factor of 1.35 before it is displayed. The reason is measurable: the version of this model that uses no local sensor at all produces a nominal 90% interval that in fact contains only 70.7% of the local record over 1,215 days, because Kandy sits outside the regime the method was validated in. A 1.35× inflation restores 90% coverage there, and the same factor is applied here, a disclosed transfer, since a daily record is the only place that deficit can be measured.

Finally, only the basin-wide level is forecast. There is no forecast of the street-scale pattern, so the map for a future hour uses the typical pattern for that month and hour from the reconstructed record. The shape of the map is climatology; the level on top of it is the forecast.

What kind of model this is, and what it is not

It is worth being exact about the physics, because the model contains a genuine dispersion solver and it would be easy to over-read what that buys.

There is real transport physics inside. The spatial pattern is generated by a terrain-aware solver of the steady-state advection–diffusion–deposition equation, u·∇C − ∇·(K∇C) + λC = S, on the valley's digital elevation model: wind channelled along the valley, night-time drainage flow pooling on the floor, diffusivity suppressed across ridges, a first-order deposition loss, and an emission source from the road network. Its three free parameters were calibrated against ten monitored valleys elsewhere, not tuned to Kandy.

But the field you see is not a solution of that equation, and we do not claim it is. Four things are true and a careful reader is entitled to know them:

  • The solve is steady-state. There is no time-derivative, so each hour is solved as if in equilibrium and the field carries no memory of previous hours. It cannot build a multi-day episode by accumulation.
  • Only the shape of the solution is used. The solved field is divided by its own mean and bounded, so the concentrations the solver produces are discarded and only the relative pattern survives.
  • The level is imposed, not conserved. The basin mean is pinned to the temporal anchor by construction. Mass conservation in the physical sense is replaced by that statistical constraint.
  • The overall framework (a regional background plus a locally structured increment ) is a source-apportionment decomposition, not a chemical transport model.
  • The transport layer has now been scored, and it does not improve the map. Across ten cities with monitoring networks, ranking neighbourhoods using the raw emission surface beats ranking them after the dispersion solver has run (+0.37 against +0.27). The solver moves contrast to places the observations do not support. It is retained because it is physically motivated and it supplies the hour-to-hour behaviour, but the headline field does not depend on it and we would not defend the displacement it produces as an accuracy gain.

So: transport physics generates the pattern; the decomposition places it; the anchor sets the level. That is a deliberate design, a full transport model would need a local emission inventory and local measurements to constrain it, and Kandy has neither, but it means the hourly spatial amplitude is a physically-motivated scenario rather than a solved quantity, and we report the annual contrast (about 1.2× core to edge) as the figure we would defend.

When the background and local split cannot be made

The map separates a regional background (pollution that arrives over the valley regardless of what happens in it) from the local increment on top. That separation fails on a substantial minority of hours, and the site now says so rather than quietly showing a flat map.

The reason is a resolution mismatch. The total is estimated hourly and swings widely through the day; the background is estimated at daily resolution and does not. On well-ventilated hours the total falls below the day's estimated background, which leaves no increment to distribute across the map. This happens in about 28% of all hours, concentrated between April and December when the basin is cleanest, roughly 2–13% of hours in January–March, rising to 27–48% from April. What this means, stated correctly. It does not mean local emissions stopped. Traffic, cooking and waste burning continue every hour of every day, so at any emitting location the local increment is strictly positive at all times, including on rainy days, when emissions carry on and only the removal changes. A background that exceeds the total is therefore not a physical state; it is a sign that the background estimate is too high for that hour. The honest reading is that the hourly split is unavailable there, with the local share known only to be above zero. The site now says that, and refuses to print a percentage, rather than printing a zero that would misdescribe the atmosphere. The map itself still carries the local pattern: the served field applies a ventilated-hour pattern floor, so it is not flat.

We tried to fix it rather than describe it. Five reformulations of the background were built and tested: bounding it against the total with the annual mass preserved; rebuilding it from local dilution and washout physics; adding the diurnal response it lacks; setting its level from an island-wide reference network; and estimating it as the persistent lower envelope of the total. Each one was rejected by a measurement. The obstruction is that four requirements, a background with its own dynamics, a background that never exceeds the total, a regional-versus-local split inside the range the published literature supports, and the observed wet-versus-dry seasonal contrast, cannot all hold at once with the data available.

What would resolve it is not a better model. About 2% of hours put the modelled total below the regional floor measured by a national network, and that network's stations are coastal towns while Kandy is inland and elevated. A single monitor in the basin, or upwind of it, would establish which of the two is wrong and remove the ambiguity. Until then the annual and seasonal figures (which do not depend on this split) stand, and the hourly split is reported only where it can be made.

Uncertainty

Every field carries a calibrated 90% interval. The map has an upper-bound toggle, and each numeric readout (basin, core, point queries, health figures) shows its interval next to the central value. The intervals come from conformal calibration on 2019–2023, the same period the explorer serves. Health figures use the GEMM exposure-response function and are always shown with their 95% interval.

Where the map puts the highest values

The readouts distinguish the city centre (the fixed grid cell over central Kandy) from the peak (wherever the highest value sits that hour). They often differ, and the difference is physics rather than noise:

  • Rush hours: emission timing and a shallow morning boundary layer concentrate the local increment near the centre, which then usually holds the peak.
  • Midday: deep convective mixing flattens the field. Differences between centre and basin shrink to a fraction of a µg/m³, well inside the interval, so their ordering carries no information at those hours.
  • Night: terrain drainage (katabatic flow) can carry the accumulated local increment down-valley, so the night-time maximum may sit away from the centre. This displacement is a prediction of the wind model rather than a numerical artefact — but it is unverified, and the layer that produces it does not improve neighbourhood ranking where we can check it (see above). Treat a displaced night-time peak as a hypothesis the model offers, not a finding.
  • Haze events: the elevated regional background covers the whole basin uniformly by construction; the model never concentrates transported background into the city core. What does concentrate is the locally-generated increment, because the same calm, shallow-boundary-layer conditions that let regional haze linger also trap local emissions. The decomposition panel separates the two parts for any hour.

Documented events

Three documented Kandy haze events are provided as presets: November 2019, December 2022 (island-wide advisories) and February–March 2023 (inter-monsoon). The model reconstructs elevated levels on all three dates through the same mechanism in each case: a high regional background under a calm, shallow boundary layer.

Weather data

The weather panel shows ERA5 reanalysis conditions for the selected hour (temperature, humidity, rain, wind, boundary-layer height). These are the same meteorological inputs the model uses, shown for context; they are historical reanalysis values, not live observations.
